Clef carries structure through compilation: a value's dimension, its grade, its lifetime, and its escape behavior are facts the compiler knows and preserves, not properties hoped for at runtime. The Adaptive Domain Model extends that discipline to learned systems. Where a domain has structure known with certainty before any training example arrives, a conserved grade, a physical dimension, an equivariance under a known group, an ADM expresses that structure in the type system, enforces it during training, and discharges it with a verifier, so the trained model holds the structure exactly rather than as a statistical average. This is the contribution the section is built on, and it is argued in full in the ADM pre-print.

The reason it matters is a utility argument, and the section leads with it. A typed domain model is more precise, because a structure held as a type cannot drift the way a learned regularity does, and an inference that depends on it is correct by construction rather than correct on average. It is cheaper to run on simpler hardware, because a model that holds its structure as fact does not spend parameters discovering that structure or representing the interactions its domain forbids; it is smaller and known-sparse, which is what modest hardware wants. And it makes the whole system faster in interaction, because it divides labor that a monolithic transformer is otherwise forced to carry alone. The domain models handle the parts where correctness can be guaranteed, and the large language model is relieved of competence it held only weakly and expensively.

That division leaves one node to account for. Something must still interface with the unstructured world, with natural-language intent, underspecified goals, and partial programs that carry no domain type. The language model is that node, and it is the one node of the constellation that cannot wear an ADM type, because the prior structure of language admits no compact formal specification, the precise boundary the pre-print draws around its own method. The section leads with the typed contribution and arrives at the language model as the interface that contribution relieves and contains, rather than treating the language model as the subject and the typed models as an afterthought.

One thread of the utility argument runs far enough to deserve naming here, because it recurs across several articles: the sub-quadratic story. The field's escape from attention's quadratic cost, the linear-attention and state-space families whose current frontier is Mamba-3, works by replacing all-pairs attention with a learned generator. Notably, that frontier has converged on the same complex-valued rotational generator the framework types, reintroducing complex dynamics the lineage had abandoned and bridging them to RoPE; the framework arrives at the same object by construction rather than by experiment, and holds its structure exact where the field's version drifts under ordinary training. So the framework buys the sub-quadratic speed without the structural corruption, and addresses by construction the two problems the newest work still lists as open: state tracking, and the gap between linear-in-theory and efficient-in-hardware inference. Sub-quadratic in context, lean in parameters, and sparse in structure are three independent reductions that compound on simple hardware; the articles on architecture, the constellation, and reversible cores each pick up this thread where it touches their subject.

One body of external work informs how that language node is built, and the section keeps it named and present throughout rather than citing it once and moving on. In *Principles and Practice of Deep Representation Learning, or A Mathematical Theory of Memory* (2026), available in full as a [navigable web edition](https://ma-lab-berkeley.github.io/deep-representation-learning-book/), Sam Buchanan, Druv Pai, Peng Wang, and Yi Ma set out a decade of results from Ma's group under a single thesis: a deep network is an unrolled optimization, each layer one step of an algorithm descending an information-theoretic objective, and a learned representation is a memory, a compact encoding of a low-dimensional distribution as a union of incoherent subspaces. Their CRATE architecture is the worked result, derived block by block rather than searched for, with a recent causal variant for sequences; its [reference implementation](https://github.com/Ma-Lab-Berkeley/CRATE) is open.

The section's engagement with that book is its spine, and it names both an alignment and a divergence. The alignment: the book and the Fidelity Framework arrive at the same target geometry, a representation as a union of low-dimensional, non-interacting subspaces, the book by optimization and the framework by construction. The divergence is more consequential, and it is a divergence in *reading*, not only in result, and not a matter of one reader being more capable than another. The common reading takes the book as an interpretability result and builds CRATE on the dense-tensor substrate the field has standardized on, which has no way to record the structure the derivation proves, so the structure thins out in the artifact. A reading informed by the framework's underpinnings, dimensional and grade types, geometric algebra, the Program Hypergraph, and b-posit with the quire, takes the same mathematics as a specification to be enforced rather than a behavior to be approximated. The two readings produce different artifacts from one text, and the difference is the substrate, not the reader. *Architecture and Arithmetic* states this divergence in full, by named section of the book and with a concrete reframing of the published CRATE operator, and the other articles carry it forward; wherever the book is cited from there, the framework-informed reading is meant, and it is offered as an invitation to anyone whose substrate can support it.

The section is eight articles, and they are written to be entered from any direction; the descriptions below are a map, not a required path. *Why Adaptive Domain Models Change the Inference Problem* establishes the contribution and the utility argument above, with a highlight reel of the type-theoretic case and the pre-print cited in full, and is the natural place to begin for the motivation. *A Scaffold for Constrained Models* names the three commitments that carry the language node when the type scaffold cannot apply. *Building the Constrained Language Model* treats its tuning: two passes, a damping taxonomy that protects the compiler's own output, and the deterministic constraint layer that supplies the node's only hard guarantee. *Architecture and Arithmetic* works through the derived backbone and the b-posit and quire discipline that makes the structure it converges toward sharp instead of approximate. *Forward-Mode and Low-Rank Adaptation* is where the efficiency claim is paid for, gradients taken over the derived structure rather than an undifferentiated parameter space. *The Constellation* is the keystone, showing the porous language node and the typed domain models composed into one system, with the boundary between emergent and constructed structure located on a concrete subsystem. *Reversible Cores and Inference-Time Recall* opens a frontier, reframing the framework's negative and fractional types as a design theory of reversible computation inside a model. And *Adapting Inference on a Gradient* is the practical counterpart, the adoption path that lets an organization run the constellation today with an existing model and adapt, along a gradient, toward one built to fit it, using the same staged-substitution strategy the compiler uses for its backends.

The status throughout is speculative. The ADM contribution is argued in its pre-print, but the constellation this section describes, the typed models composed with the language node on a shared substrate, is a research program, not a shipped system. The articles say so plainly and mark the open questions that could end the program, including the experiments that have not been run. The aim is to show that the typed domain model is the source of the precision, the hardware economy, and the divided workload, and that a language node can be built to complete the constellation without claiming the formal guarantees the language domain does not admit. If you have read the ADM pre-print and are wondering how a language model fits into a framework built on types it cannot wear, the first article is a good entry point, though any of them will stand on its own.
